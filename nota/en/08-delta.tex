\section{A Dirac delta off the real axis}
\label{sec:delta}

\texttt{delta.dfw} (July 2010) is the most ambitious notebook of the
collection. It takes an elementary family that approximates the Dirac delta,
evaluates it at complex points, and uses the result to give a value to the
Fourier transform of a growing exponential. The computations are correct;
what does not hold up is the interpretation. This section explains why, and
leaves open the question the notebook raises.

\subsection{The family and its complex limit}

Lines \righe{delta.dfw}{1}{3} use, with $a=1/(2n)$,
\[
  \delta_n(x)=\frac14\left[\frac{1+2nx}{|x+a|}+\frac{1-2nx}{|x-a|}\right]
  =\frac n2\bigl[\sgn(x+a)-\sgn(x-a)\bigr],
\]
since $1\pm2nx=\pm2n(x\pm a)$. For real $x$ this is a rectangle of height $n$
and base $1/n$, that is of area $1$, written with two sign functions and no
piecewise definition. The formula also makes sense for complex $z$, with
$\sgn z=z/|z|$, and it is a difference quotient with step $1/n$:
\[
  \delta_n(z)=\frac12\,\frac{\sgn(z+a)-\sgn(z-a)}{2a}.
\]
Hence, for $z\neq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deltacomplessa}
  \lim_{n\to\infty}\delta_n(z)=\frac12\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\,\frac{z}{|z|}
  =\frac{z\,\Imag z}{2\ii\,|z|^3},
\end{equation}
where we used $\partial_x(z/|z|)=(z\bar z-zx)/|z|^3=-\ii yz/|z|^3$ with
$z=x+\ii y$. This is the formula of \riga{delta.dfw}{4}. The limit function,
which we denote by $\delta(z)$, vanishes on the real axis away from the
origin, satisfies $\delta(az)=\delta(z)/|a|$ for every nonzero real $a$, like
the true delta (\righe{delta.dfw}{8}{10}), and obeys
$\delta(z)+\delta(\ii z)=1/(2|z|)$ (line \#6). So far everything is correct.

\subsection{The limit depends on the family}

On the real line, the limit of an approximating family does not depend on the
family: they all define the same distribution. Off the line this is no longer
true. With the Lorentzian family
\[
  L_\varepsilon(z)=\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi(z^2+\varepsilon^2)}
\]
the limit as $\varepsilon\to0$ is $0$ at every $z\neq0$. With the Gaussian
family
\[
  G_\varepsilon(z)=\frac{\ee^{-z^2/\varepsilon^2}}{\varepsilon\sqrt\pi},
  \qquad |G_\varepsilon(z)|=\frac{\ee^{-(x^2-y^2)/\varepsilon^2}}{\varepsilon\sqrt\pi},
\]
the limit is $0$ where $|x|>|y|$ and infinite where $|y|>|x|$. Three families
that converge to the same delta on the line give, off the line, three
different results: the value in \eqref{eq:deltacomplessa} is a property of
the rectangle, not of the delta.

This affects the result the notebook cares about most. Formally
$\int\ee^{kt}\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\,\dd t=2\pi\,\delta(\omega+\ii k)$, and lines
\righe{delta.dfw}{22}{27} compute this ``transform of the growing
exponential'' with \eqref{eq:deltacomplessa}: for instance its modulus is
$1/\bigl(2(\omega^2+1)\bigr)$ for $k=1$ (line \#24), a Lorentzian. With the
Lorentzian family the same transform would be $0$; with the Gaussian one it
would not exist. The consistency checks of lines \#48--\#51 use only
linearity, and hold for any family.

\subsection{Where the question makes sense}

There are theories in which a delta ``at a complex point'' has a precise
meaning. The Fourier transforms of compactly supported test functions are
entire functions, and on this space the transform of $\ee^{kt}$ is a
well-defined functional: evaluation of the test functions at a point of the
imaginary axis~\cite{gelfand1964}. In Sato's theory of hyperfunctions the
delta is the jump across the real axis of the holomorphic function
$F(z)=-1/(2\pi\ii z)$: by the Sokhotski--Plemelj formula
$\frac{1}{x\mp\ii0}=\mathrm{p.v.}\frac1x\pm\ii\pi\delta(x)$ one has
$F(x+\ii0)-F(x-\ii0)=\delta(x)$~\cite{kaneko1988}. In neither theory does the
delta have pointwise values off the axis: in the first it is a functional, in
the second it is defined only up to an entire function.

A question remains, one the notebook raises without knowing it.
\eqref{eq:deltacomplessa} is a natural object --- half the derivative along
the real axis of the complex sign $z/|z|$ --- and it is the only one of the
three limits that is neither zero nor infinite; it has the scaling law of the
delta. But it is not holomorphic, and nothing in the theory of distributions
singles it out. Is there a sense in which the rectangle family is the right
one? We do not know, and we do not claim it. Finally, lines
\righe{delta.dfw}{41}{44} rewrite $\ee^{2\ii x}$ with the half-angle tangent:
it is the formula of Section~\ref{sec:semiangolo}.
