\section{A chain of factorials that breaks}
\label{sec:fattoriali}

The notebook \texttt{factor.dfw} (August 2010) tests an equality between the
product of the factorials of the odd numbers and the factorial of a
triangular number. With $T_m=m(m+1)/2$ and
\[
  P_p=\prod_{k=0}^{p}(2k+1)!=1!\,3!\,5!\cdots(2p+1)!,
\]
the question is whether $P_p=T_{p+1}!$. For $p=0,1,2,3$ the answer is yes:
\[
  1!=1!,\qquad 1!\,3!=3!,\qquad 1!\,3!\,5!=6!,\qquad 1!\,3!\,5!\,7!=10!.
\]
At the fifth case the equality fails, and instead of dropping it the notebook
measures how badly it fails: lines \righe{factor.dfw}{5}{15} factor the
difference $P_p-T_{p+1}!$ up to $p=14$ (line \#5 contains a typo, see
Section~\ref{sec:refusi}). This section explains why the chain holds four
times and proves that it never closes again.

\subsection{One step at a time}

Since $P_p=P_{p-1}\,(2p+1)!$ and $T_{p+1}=T_p+p+1$, if the chain holds at
step $p-1$ it holds at step $p$ if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:passo}
  T_p!\,(2p+1)!=T_{p+1}!,
  \qquad\text{that is}\qquad
  \prod_{j=1}^{p+1}(T_p+j)=(2p+1)!.
\end{equation}
Each step is therefore an instance of the equation $a!\,b!=c!$. This has two
families of trivial solutions: the one with $a=1$, and the one with $c=b!$
and $a=c-1$, since $(b!-1)!\,b!=(b!)!$. The first steps of the chain fall
exactly there:
\[
  p=1:\ 1!\,3!=3!,\qquad
  p=2:\ 3!\,5!=6!\ \ (\text{with }3!=6).
\]
The third step, however, is
\[
  p=3:\qquad 6!\,7!=10!,
\]
which is \emph{the only known nontrivial solution} of $a!\,b!=c!$. That it is
the only one at all is a conjecture attributed to
Sur\'anyi~\cite{erdos373}, and it is problem B23 in Guy's
collection~\cite{guy2004}; there are no other solutions with
$c\le10^{3000}$~\cite{erdos373}, and partial results in this direction can be
found in~\cite{hajdu2018}. The regularity that the notebook saw last for four
steps hides no law, then: it is made of two trivial coincidences and of the
most famous nontrivial coincidence among factorials.

At the next step, \eqref{eq:passo} asks for
$11\cdot12\cdot13\cdot14\cdot15=9!$, but the left-hand side is $360{,}360$
and the right-hand side $362{,}880$: a near miss, with ratio $0.99306$.

\subsection{The chain never closes again}

\begin{prop}\label{prop:fattoriali}
For every $p\ge4$ we have $P_p>T_{p+1}!$, and the ratio $P_p/T_{p+1}!$
increases with $p$. In particular, the equality $P_p=T_{p+1}!$ holds exactly
for $p=0,1,2,3$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Let
\[
  a_p=\frac{T_{p+1}!}{T_p!\,(2p+1)!}=\frac{1}{(2p+1)!}\prod_{j=1}^{p+1}(T_p+j),
\]
so that $P_p/T_{p+1}!=(P_{p-1}/T_p!)/a_p$. Since $P_3=T_4!$, it suffices to
show that $a_p<1$ for every $p\ge4$.

For $4\le p\le100$ this is checked by exact integer arithmetic: the largest
value is $a_4=0.99306$.

For $p\ge100$ we estimate the ratio of consecutive terms. From
$T_{p+1}=T_p+p+1$ and $T_{p+1}+p+2=T_{p+2}$ it follows that
\[
  \frac{a_{p+1}}{a_p}
  =\frac{T_{p+2}}{(2p+2)(2p+3)}\prod_{j=1}^{p+1}\Bigl(1+\frac{p+1}{T_p+j}\Bigr)
  \le g(p)\,\exp\frac{(p+1)^2}{T_p+1},
  \qquad
  g(p)=\frac{(p+2)(p+3)}{4(p+1)(2p+3)},
\]
where we used $1+x\le\ee^{x}$. We have
$(p+1)^2/(T_p+1)=2(p+1)^2/(p^2+p+2)\le2+2/p\le2.02$ for $p\ge100$, and $g$
is decreasing (the numerator of its derivative is $-5p^2-18p-15$) with
$g(100)=10506/82012<0.12811$. Hence
\[
  \frac{a_{p+1}}{a_p}\le \ee^{2.02}\cdot0.12811<0.966
  \qquad (p\ge100),
\]
and $a_p<a_{100}<1$ for every $p>100$.
\end{proof}

The finite part of the proof, the $97$ exact comparisons, is carried out by
the script \texttt{verifica\_nota.py} in the repository, which also checks
the estimate. The
threshold $100$ is not special: sharper estimates would lower it, but there
is no need. The ratio $a_{p+1}/a_p$ tends to $\ee^2/8=0.9236\dots$, because
the product tends to $\ee^2$ and $g(p)$ to $1/8$: from $p=4$ on, $a_p$
decreases geometrically (Figure~\ref{fig:nota_fattoriali}), and the ratio
$P_p/T_{p+1}!$, the product of the reciprocals $1/a_q$, grows faster than
exponentially.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{nota_fattoriali}
  \caption{The ratio $a_p$ for $1\le p\le60$, on a logarithmic scale. It is

  \label{fig:nota_fattoriali}
\end{figure}

The notebook had reached the same conclusion by another route: the
factorizations of $P_p-T_{p+1}!$ contain, next to the small primes of the
factorials, large and seemingly random primes --- $7103$ already appears at $p=6$, and
$3{,}116{,}563$ and $333{,}915{,}299$ at $p=9$ --- a sign that the two sides have no
arithmetic kinship beyond the initial coincidence. The proposition says the
same thing quantitatively.

\paragraph{What remains open.}
Sur\'anyi's conjecture remains open, and the proposition does not touch it: it
only shows that the family $a=T_p$, $b=2p+1$, $c=T_{p+1}$ yields no solutions
of $a!\,b!=c!$ beyond those of the first three steps.
