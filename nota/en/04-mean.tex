\section{The mean and the midpoint}
\label{sec:media}

The last notebook of the collection, \texttt{Trasformata T.dfw} (January
2011), applies the same operator to fourteen functions,
\[
  T[f](t)=\frac{\dfrac1t\displaystyle\int_0^t f(x)\,\dd x}{f(t/2)},
\]
that is, the ratio between the mean of $f$ over $[0,t]$ and the value of $f$
at the midpoint. For an affine function the ratio is $1$. Lines
\righe{Trasformata T.dfw}{3}{8} find
\begin{gather*}
  T[\sin]=T[\cos]=T[\ee^{\ii x}]=\frac{\sin(t/2)}{t/2},\qquad
  T[\ee^{2\pi\ii x}]=\frac{\sin(\pi t)}{\pi t},\\
  T[\ee^{x}]=\frac{\sinh(t/2)}{t/2},\qquad
  T[x^n]=\frac{2^n}{n+1}.
\end{gather*}
Three different functions give the same cardinal sine, and the notebook
records it without comment. The reason is the following.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:media}
Let $f$ be of class $C^2$ with $f''=\mu^2 f$ for a complex constant $\mu$.
Then, for every interval $[c-h,c+h]$ in the domain,
\[
  \frac1{2h}\int_{c-h}^{c+h}f(x)\,\dd x=f(c)\,\frac{\sinh(\mu h)}{\mu h},
\]
where the factor equals $1$ for $\mu=0$. In particular, wherever
$f(t/2)\neq0$, $T[f](t)=\sinh(\mu t/2)/(\mu t/2)$: the cardinal sine
$\sin(t/2)/(t/2)$ for every solution of $f''=-f$, its hyperbolic analogue for
every solution of $f''=f$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
If $\mu\neq0$, $f=A\ee^{\mu x}+B\ee^{-\mu x}$. The mean of $\ee^{\mu x}$ over
$[c-h,c+h]$ is $\ee^{\mu c}(\ee^{\mu h}-\ee^{-\mu h})/(2\mu h)
=\ee^{\mu c}\sinh(\mu h)/(\mu h)$; the factor is even in $\mu$, so the same
holds for $\ee^{-\mu x}$, and linearity concludes. If $\mu=0$, $f$ is affine
and its mean equals its value at the midpoint.
\end{proof}

\begin{oss}
The converse also holds, in the following form: if $f\in C^2$ and the mean of
$f$ over $[c-h,c+h]$ equals $\varphi(h)\,f(c)$ for every $c$, with $\varphi$
independent of $c$, then $f''=\lambda f$ for some constant $\lambda$. Indeed
the mean is $f(c)+f''(c)h^2/6+o(h^2)$; choosing $c_0$ with $f(c_0)\neq0$, it
follows that $(\varphi(h)-1)/h^2$ has a limit $L$, and then $f''(c)=6L\,f(c)$
for every $c$. The functions for which the ratio between mean and central
value depends only on the width of the interval are thus exactly the
solutions of $f''=\lambda f$: this is the structure that lines \#3 to \#7
bring to light.
\end{oss}

Powers are a different case: $T[x^n]$ is constant by homogeneity, and the same
computation gives $2^\alpha/(\alpha+1)$ for every $f(x)=x^\alpha$ with
$\alpha>-1$. For the other functions in the notebook --- logarithms,
arctangent, arcsine, the Gaussian, the sine integral --- $T[f]$ depends on $t$
without an equally simple structure.

\paragraph{Context.}
The proposition is the one-dimensional case of the mean value theorem for the
Helmholtz equation. If $\Delta u+k^2u=0$ in a neighbourhood of the closed ball
with centre $x$ and radius $r$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the mean $M(r)$ of $u$ over
the sphere of radius $r$ satisfies $M''+\frac{n-1}{r}M'+k^2M=0$, with
$M(0)=u(x)$ and $M'(0)=0$ (Darboux's equation for spherical
means~\cite{john1955}), and therefore
\[
  M(r)=u(x)\,\Gamma\Bigl(\frac n2\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{2}{kr}\Bigr)^{n/2-1}
  J_{n/2-1}(kr).
\]
For $n=1$ the ``sphere'' is the pair of points $c\pm r$ and
$M(r)=u(c)\cos(kr)$; averaging over the radius gives the mean over the
interval, $u(c)\sin(kr)/(kr)$, which is the proposition with $\mu=\ii k$. For
$n=3$ one gets $M(r)=u(x)\sin(kr)/(kr)$: the mean over a segment in dimension
one has the same kernel as the mean over a sphere in dimension three.
