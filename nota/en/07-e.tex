\section{Two sequences for \texorpdfstring{$\ee$}{e}}
\label{sec:e}

The notebook \texttt{e.dfw} (August 2010, ninety-three lines) takes the
definition $(1+1/n)^n$ and modifies it in many ways to see what survives. One
of the modifications replaces $1+1/n$ with the truncated geometric series:
\[
  \lim_{n\to\infty}\Bigl(\sum_{k=0}^{n}n^{-k}\Bigr)^{n}=\ee
\]
(\righe{e.dfw}{19}{32} and \righe{e.dfw}{70}{87}). Derive returns $1$ as the
limit (lines \#30 and \#71), which is wrong. The notebook notices by
evaluating the sequence at $n=9{,}999{,}999$, where it finds a distance from
$\ee$ of $1.359141174\cdot10^{-7}$ (lines \#72--\#75): the value is correct
in all the digits shown.

Since $\sum_{k=0}^{n}n^{-k}=(1-n^{-n-1})/(1-1/n)$, the sequence differs from
$(1-1/n)^{-n}$ by the factor $(1-n^{-n-1})^n$, which is closer to $1$ than
any power of $1/n$. In practice the notebooks' sequence is
$(1-1/n)^{-n}=\bigl(n/(n-1)\bigr)^n$, that is, the classical one with $-n$ in
place of $n$ (\righe{e.dfw}{77}{80}).

\begin{prop}\label{prop:e}
As $n\to\infty$,
\begin{align*}
  \Bigl(1+\frac1n\Bigr)^{n}&=\ee\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{11}{24n^2}+O(n^{-3})\Bigr),\\
  \Bigl(1-\frac1n\Bigr)^{-n}&=\ee\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{11}{24n^2}+O(n^{-3})\Bigr),
\end{align*}
and the mean of the two is $\ee\bigl(1+\frac{11}{24n^2}+O(n^{-4})\bigr)$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
We have
\[
  n\ln\Bigl(1+\frac1n\Bigr)=1-\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{3n^2}+O(n^{-3}),
\]
and hence, since $\frac13+\frac18=\frac{11}{24}$,
\[
  \Bigl(1+\frac1n\Bigr)^{n}=\ee\exp\Bigl(-\frac1{2n}+\frac1{3n^2}+O(n^{-3})\Bigr)
  =\ee\Bigl(1-\frac1{2n}+\frac{11}{24n^2}+O(n^{-3})\Bigr).
\]
The second sequence is the first evaluated at $-n$, since
$(1+\frac{1}{-n})^{-n}=(1-\frac1n)^{-n}$: its expansion in powers of $1/n$ is
the same with the sign of the odd terms changed, and these cancel in the
mean.
\end{proof}

At $n=10^7$ the errors are $-1.35914\cdot10^{-7}$ for the classical sequence
and $+1.35914\cdot10^{-7}$ for the notebooks' one: same size, opposite sign.
The notebooks' sequence does not converge faster than the classical one; it
converges at the same rate, from the other side. The real gain lies in the
mean, whose error is $1.2459\cdot10^{-14}$, equal to $11\ee/(24n^2)$ in all
the digits shown (Figure~\ref{fig:nota_e_errori}). It is the same mechanism
by which the central difference approximates a derivative better than the
forward difference: combining a value with its mirror image removes the odd
terms of the error.

The notebook closes (\righe{e.dfw}{88}{93}) by replacing $1/x$ with
$\ii\pi/x$:
\[
  \Bigl(1+\frac{\ii\pi}{x}\Bigr)^{x}\to-1,\qquad
  \Bigl(1+\frac{2\pi\ii}{x}\Bigr)^{x}\to1,\qquad
  \Bigl(1+\frac{\ii\pi}{2x}\Bigr)^{x}\to\ii,
\]
and in general $(1+\ii x/k)^k\to\ee^{\ii x}=\cos x+\ii\sin x$: Euler's
formula obtained as a limit, which is the classical route.
