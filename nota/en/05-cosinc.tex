\section{The cardinal sine and its twin}
\label{sec:cosinc}

The notebook \texttt{cosinc.dfw} (November 2010) studies a single function,
\[
  F(x)=\frac{(-1)^x-1}{\pi x}.
\]
With the principal determination declared in the file header,
$(-1)^x=\ee^{\ii\pi x}$, and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cosinc}
  F(x)=\frac{\ee^{\ii\pi x}-1}{\pi x}=\ii\int_0^1\ee^{\ii\pi xs}\,\dd s .
\end{equation}
Lines \righe{cosinc.dfw}{6}{11} and \riga{cosinc.dfw}{28} observe that the
ratio between the derivative of $(-1)^x$ and $\pi(-1)^x$ equals $\ii$, and
that $F(0)=\lim_{h\to0}F(h)=\ii$: the imaginary unit as a ratio of
derivatives. This is the correct answer to the question that \texttt{j.dfw}
had asked sixteen months earlier (Section~\ref{sec:breve}).

Lines \righe{cosinc.dfw}{14}{16} expand $F$ as a series,
$F(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}\ii^{n+1}(\pi x)^n/(n+1)!$, and separate its two parts:
\[
  \Imag F(x)=\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x},\qquad
  \Real F(x)=\frac{\cos\pi x-1}{\pi x}.
\]
The first is the cardinal sine. The second, with its sign changed, is the
``twin'' of the title: it oscillates with the same period and decays at the
same rate (Figure~\ref{fig:nota_cosinc}), but it is odd rather than even, and
under integration it behaves in the opposite way.

\subsection{The twin is the Hilbert transform}

With the convention
\[
  Hf(x)=\frac1\pi\,\mathrm{p.v.}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f(t)}{x-t}\,\dd t ,
\]
the Hilbert transform multiplies every component $\ee^{\ii\omega x}$ by
$-\ii\sgn\omega$~\cite{king2009}. Since
$\sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)=\frac12\int_{-1}^{1}\ee^{\ii\pi xs}\,\dd s$, we get
\[
  H\Bigl[\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\Bigr]
  =-\frac\ii2\Bigl(\int_0^1-\int_{-1}^0\Bigr)\ee^{\ii\pi xs}\,\dd s
  =\frac{1-\cos\pi x}{\pi x},
\]
and hence, by \eqref{eq:cosinc},
\[
  \frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}+\ii\,H\Bigl[\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\Bigr]
  =\int_0^1\ee^{\ii\pi xs}\,\dd s,
  \qquad
  F(x)=\ii\Bigl(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}+\ii\,H\Bigl[\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\Bigr]\Bigr).
\]
The twin is therefore the Hilbert transform of the cardinal sine; together
they form the \emph{analytic signal} of the cardinal sine, which is the
Fourier transform of the one-sided rectangle $[0,1]$ instead of the symmetric
one $[-1,1]$. The notebook's function is this analytic signal multiplied by
$\ii$.

\subsection{Two opposite integrals}

The cardinal sine has integral $1$ over the whole line
(\riga{cosinc.dfw}{19}). The twin, instead, is not integrable: for $x>0$ it
is nonnegative, and over each period its mean is $1/(\pi x)$ up to
higher-order terms, so $\int_0^X(1-\cos\pi x)/(\pi x)\,\dd x$ grows like
$(\ln X)/\pi$. The value $11.534$ that Derive returns at line \#20, and its
half computed at lines \#21 and \#22, are numerical artifacts: the integral
diverges logarithmically.

The phenomenon is typical of the Hilbert transform. For a function $f$ that
decays fast enough, $Hf(x)$ behaves like $\frac{1}{\pi x}\int f$ for large
$|x|$~\cite{king2009}, and cannot be integrable if $\int f\neq0$. The
cardinal sine decays too slowly for the result to apply literally, but the
conclusion is the same: on average the twin decays like $1/(\pi x)$, that is,
exactly like $\frac{1}{\pi x}\int\frac{\sin\pi t}{\pi t}\,\dd t$.

\paragraph{What remains open.}
Nothing: the Hilbert transform of the cardinal sine and its analytic signal are
known; the notebook reaches them by another route.
